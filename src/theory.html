PVD basics

A thin film is a coating on a supporting material called the substrate. In physical vapour deposition (PVD), coating material leaves a source as atoms or ions, travels through a low-pressure chamber and accumulates on the substrate. An ion is an atom or molecule with an electrical charge. A plasma is a gas containing free electrons and ions.

Evaporation
Heat releases atoms from the source.
Sputtering
Ion impacts eject atoms from a solid target.
Ion plating
Ions strike the substrate while coating material arrives.

Chamber and substrate

Pressure and particle flight

Base pressure is the pressure reached after pumping the chamber down, before coating or admitting a process gas. Working pressure is the pressure during deposition. Sputter coaters typically admit argon while the pump keeps running, so the pressure settles as gas inflow balances removal. Reactive methods also admit a gas such as nitrogen or oxygen. The evaporation presets do not deliberately admit a process gas. In ion beam assisted deposition, argon is metered into the ion gun.

The mean free path, λ, is the average distance between collisions in the gas. Higher pressure means a shorter λ. Compare it with the source–substrate gap, L. For L much smaller than λ, most atoms cross the gap directly. If L is several times λ, collisions broaden the range of arrival angles and can reduce the flux that reaches the wafer.

Transport model and animation

The app estimates the mean free path with λ = kBT/(√2 πd2p), where kB is the Boltzmann constant and p is the working pressure. It uses argon at 300 K with diameter d = 0.34 nm as a reference. The direct fraction is exp(−L/λ) and scattered collection uses the illustrative factor 1/(1 + L/(8λ)). The chamber animation slows flights, discharges and valve transients for viewing. Film growth calculations use the selected working pressure and separate rates.

Temperature and electrical connections

A resistance heater behind the holder sets the substrate temperature Ts. Warmer surfaces often let newly arrived atoms move farther before they become trapped in the growing film. The holder thermocouple is a proxy for the substrate temperature. The two can differ in a real chamber. Each selectable substrate has a thermal reference with a stated basis. For example, a glass strain point and a polymer service limit describe different behaviours.

Follow the electrical leads in the chamber to find the powered electrode and its return. A negatively biased electrode attracts positive ions. In a biased holder, the heater circuit has its own leads and is electrically isolated from the bias supply. In electron beam evaporation, the negative emitter sends electrons towards the grounded hearth.

Evaporation

Heating raises the material's equilibrium vapour pressure, pv. This is the pressure of vapour in equilibrium with the solid or liquid at a given temperature. In a pumped chamber, vapour escapes into the surrounding vacuum and some of it condenses on the cooler substrate. A solid can supply vapour by sublimation. The bulk melting point marks a change of phase, while the vapour-pressure curve helps decide whether the source will supply enough material.

In direct resistance heating, electric current heats the coating material itself. A heated boat transfers heat to a separate charge. A radiant heater transfers heat across a gap, and an electron beam concentrates energy on material in a hearth. Open Source to compare vapour pressure and the melting point for the selected material.

Vapour-pressure model and silver benchmark

The Hertz–Knudsen source flux is pv/√(2πmkBT), where m is the mass of one atom and T is the source temperature in kelvin. The model interpolates log10 pv against 1/T from tabulated anchors. It assumes unit evaporation and sticking coefficients, a cosine emitter and effective source areas of 0.6–2.4 cm².

Kurt J. Lesker reports Ag deposition of 0.1–0.5 nm/s at about 1373 K and a base pressure at or below 10−6 Torr. The model's Ag boat gives about 0.37 nm/s under comparable conditions. The rate measured in a particular coater also depends on geometry, source loading and sensor position.

Sputtering

Argon ions accelerate towards a negatively biased target and transfer momentum to its atoms. Some atoms escape from the surface. The sputtering yield, Y, is the average number of atoms ejected per incident ion. Raising the ion energy can increase Y. Raising the ion current increases the number of impacts per second. The Source tab shows the yield, while Growth also reflects transport and collection at the substrate.

In a DC diode, the target and chamber support the discharge directly. A magnetron uses a magnetic field B to keep electrons near the target longer. These electrons create more argon ions close to the target, often at lower working pressure than a conventional diode. In pulsed DC, brief intervals help discharge patches where charge builds up. Radio-frequency (RF) power can sustain sputtering of an insulating target, such as silica. The RF target develops a negative time-averaged bias.

Reactive coatings and ITO

Adding nitrogen to a titanium sputtering discharge can form titanium nitride (TiN). Nitride can also form on the target, lowering the yield as more of its surface becomes compound-covered. This is called target poisoning. Similarly, reactive sputtering can oxidise an indium–tin alloy to form indium tin oxide (ITO), a transparent conductor. A second industrial route uses a conductive ITO ceramic target, often with a small oxygen addition to tune the film.

Yield and composition in the simulation

For singly charged Ar⁺, the target ejects approximately IiY/e atoms per second, with ion current Ii and elementary charge e. The model interpolates metal yields over its energy range and uses effective ion energy and current for RF. The silica target uses a simplified effective yield.

The TiN recipe models compound coverage as competing nitrogen uptake and removal by ion impacts. The covered area uses 37% of the metallic yield and the nominal film switches to TiN above 0.15 Pa N₂. The ceramic ITO reference rate is 20 nm/min. The reactive In–Sn recipe changes towards ITO near 0.030 Pa O₂ in this model. Real transition pressures depend on the coater, pumping and power. The app does not calculate stoichiometry, sheet resistance or measured optical transmittance.

Ion plating and ion assistance

Ion plating combines the arrival of coating material with energetic ion bombardment at the substrate. In classic ion plating, an evaporation source supplies material and a discharge supplies ions. A negative substrate bias accelerates positive ions through the sheath, the region where voltage drops between the plasma and the substrate.

In classic ion plating, Pre-clean runs the argon discharge with the evaporation source switched off. Ar⁺ ions can remove adsorbed molecules and sputter part of the exposed surface. Select Deposit to start film growth in the same chamber. The residue curve shows a modelled fraction of removable material. It cannot identify a contaminant or predict adhesion.

A cathodic arc releases vapour and ions from a moving spot on a titanium cathode. A nitrogen feed supplies the nitrogen for TiN. In ion beam assisted deposition (IBAD), a separate gun directs an ion beam at the substrate, so beam energy and current can be adjusted independently of the coating source.

Ion impacts can help atoms rearrange and fill voids. Energetic bombardment can also remove film atoms by resputtering. Net growth is the incoming deposition rate minus that loss, Rnet = Rin − Rloss. If loss exceeds arrival, the existing film thins until the substrate is exposed.

Ion transport and erosion assumptions

The incoming rate is specified at a 14 cm reference gap before gas attenuation. Classic plating uses a collisional 6 mm sheath estimate. The arc model uses an effective ion charge of 1.5 elementary charges and 20 eV initial energy. The erosion estimates use Ar⁺ metal yields as proxies, including 45% of the Ti yield for TiN. Ion energy and ion flux influence different parts of the response.

Film and diagnostics

Source, Growth and Profile

Source shows the response that generates or removes coating material, such as vapour pressure, sputtering yield or ion-driven loss. Growth shows how the film at the wafer centre changes over time. Profile shows thickness across the 100 mm wafer. An edge-to-centre ratio near 100% indicates a more uniform deposit in the plotted profile, regardless of the total thickness.

Sensors

A quartz crystal microbalance (QCM) infers deposited mass from how much its crystal's resonance frequency falls. The crystal sits to the side of the wafer, so a tooling factor corrects for the different collection geometry. The app models a shielded, unbiased crystal. If ions remove film at the wafer, the wafer can be thinner than the thickness the crystal indicates.

The vacuum gauge reads total pressure and the residual gas analyser (RGA) estimates the contribution from individual gases. The thermocouple represents the holder temperature. Optical emission spectroscopy (OES) looks at light from excited species. In the reactive Ti example, the relative Ti emission is a modelled proxy for target yield at fixed discharge conditions.

Sensor model assumptions

The 6 MHz QCM uses the Sauerbrey relation, Δf = −2f02(m/A)/√(ρqμq), for a thin, rigid mass load. Here m/A is deposited mass per area and ρq and μq are the quartz density and shear modulus. Bulk film density converts mass to indicated thickness. The model omits temperature drift and corrections for heavy or soft films. It represents high vacuum with a cold-cathode gauge and the working range with a capacitance diaphragm gauge, assuming differential pumping for the RGA at working pressures.

Zone model

A structure zone model maps broad trends in film structure. The homologous temperature Ts/Tm divides substrate temperature by the bulk melting temperature of the deposited material, both in kelvin. This helps compare materials whose atoms become mobile at different temperatures. Sputtering maps add gas pressure to describe how much the gas scatters incoming material. Ion-assisted maps also account for energy delivered at the surface.

The marker places the current settings on a schematic or study-specific comparison. Check Model notes for its axes and assumptions. The Anders-style view for classic ion plating and IBAD uses a simplified projection with the thermal part of T* and an Ar⁺ kinetic energy estimate. TiN, ITO and silica use comparisons suited to those coatings. Substrate and film thickness can shift actual structure trends.

Morphology

Morphology means the shape and arrangement of features in the film. Deposited atoms can start small islands, move across the surface and join existing islands. At oblique arrival angles, taller features can block incoming material from reaching lower ones. This shadowing can lead to open columns. Greater surface mobility can help fill gaps.

The SEM-style view highlights shape in grayscale. In Grains, colours mark modelled crystalline regions. A grain's reported diameter is that of a circle with the same exposed top area, in grid cells. Amorphous silica has no crystalline grains in the model. The areal RMS roughness is the root mean square deviation of the simulated surface height from its mean, with bare substrate cells counted at zero height.

What the 3D model represents

The 40 × 40 lateral grid uses coarse parcels. The evaporation model favours early island formation for silver and gold on oxides. In sputtering, collisions broaden angle distributions and reduce the contribution from energetic neutrals. In ion plating, energy delivered per arriving film unit increases local rearrangement up to a limit. The numerical loss rate removes exposed parcels. The model has no calibrated lateral grain scale, and cannot predict microscopy contrast, texture, stress or adhesion.

Chemical vapour deposition

In chemical vapour deposition (CVD), gases carry precursors, the starting chemicals for the coating. Reactions at or near the substrate produce a solid film. Volatile by-products leave with the exhaust. Depending on the process, heat or a plasma can drive the reaction. In PVD, the supply of coating material starts with physical release from a source or target, even when a reactive gas then contributes to the compound film.

Fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO, SnO₂:F) is a transparent conducting coating commonly produced by atmospheric-pressure CVD on hot float glass. It is an industrial counterpart to the transparent conducting ITO films in the PVD simulator. The interactive CVD mode is in development.
